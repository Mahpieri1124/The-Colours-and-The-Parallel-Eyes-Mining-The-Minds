## Central proposition

The research examines how simultaneous fragmentation, institutional and informational uncertainty, disruption of civilian–institutional communication channels, and distributed or shifting decision authority can create a condition of **civilian compression**: a severe, multi-dimensional constraint in which people experience insecurity, contradictory signals, reduced access to protection, weakened agency, and uncertainty about which institutions can hear, interpret, and respond to their needs.

The framework treats civilian compression as a **proposed analytical construct**, not an established clinical, legal, or universally standardized category. It asks whether and under what conditions the convergence of fragmentation, communication-bridge failure, decision-centre opacity, technology-mediated information disorder, and institutional passivity can elevate atrocity-risk conditions. It does not claim that these conditions automatically cause genocide. Genocide has a specific legal definition and requires proof of legally relevant elements, including specific intent; risk analysis must not be confused with legal determination.

## Working model

`Fragmentation + uncertainty + civilian–institutional communication failure + unclear/distributed accountability + delayed protection response`

`↕ feedback / feed-forward across households, communities, services, local authorities, national institutions, international organizations and digital infrastructures`

`→ civilian compression and protection deficits → elevated vulnerability / atrocity-risk signals → preventive action, protection, accountability and institutional learning`

This is a multi-directional systems model. Feedback may amplify harm or enable mitigation. The model must be tested against alternative explanations and contextual evidence.

## Four analytical domains

1. **Fragmentation and decision topology:** how authority, responsibility, communication, and coordination are distributed, nested, contested, or disrupted.
2. **Civilian compression and communication bridges:** how civilians reach protection, services, trusted information, humanitarian actors, and accountable decision-makers.
3. **Hybrid designation and information environments:** how classification, attribution, data gathering, narratives, and algorithmic systems may affect civilian status, trust, and institutional decisions.
4. **Prevention and institutional adaptation:** how peace, humanitarian, development, and human-rights organizations can move from warning to timely, lawful, civilian-centred action.

## Essential safeguards

- No deterministic claim that fragmentation inevitably leads to atrocity crimes.
- No claim that the framework predicts genocide or replaces the UN Framework of Analysis for Atrocity Crimes.
- No operational intelligence, infiltration, surveillance, targeting, or military guidance.
- Do not collapse civilian dissent, identity, association, digital activity, or humanitarian contact into suspicion.
- Protect privacy, informed consent, source safety, data minimization, and do-no-harm principles.
- Preserve human judgment and independent review where AI-supported assessments are used.
- Include local communities and affected populations as knowledge-holders and decision participants, not merely data sources.
