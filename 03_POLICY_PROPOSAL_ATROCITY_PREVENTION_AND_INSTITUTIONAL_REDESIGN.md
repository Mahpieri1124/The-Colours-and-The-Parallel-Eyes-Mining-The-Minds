# Policy Proposal — Civilian Protection, Atrocity Prevention, and Adaptive Redesign of Global Peace Institutions

## Executive policy proposition

**Global peace-oriented, humanitarian, and sustainable-development institutions should urgently redesign their prevention architecture so that credible civilian warnings can travel across fragmented systems, reach an accountable decision-maker, trigger timely protective consideration, and generate transparent follow-up.**

The central risk addressed here is not simply conflict escalation. It is the possibility that civilians become trapped in a condition of severe pressure and uncertainty while the communication bridges connecting them to protection institutions are damaged, inaccessible, distrusted, or unanswered. Under certain contexts, such failures may interact with established atrocity-risk factors. The resulting protection gap can be consequential in the immediate, short-term, and medium-term horizons.

This proposal treats prevention of possible genocide and other atrocity crimes as an urgent institutional responsibility. It does not claim that the proposed framework can predict genocide, determine legal classification, or replace the UN Framework of Analysis for Atrocity Crimes. It proposes a practical complement: a civilian-centred early-warning-to-early-action system with explicit responsibility, response timelines, safeguards, and independent review.

## 1. The urgency: passivity is not a neutral position

When credible civilian warnings accumulate but institutions remain passive, fragmented, under-resourced, or unable to coordinate, the gap between warning and protection can widen. Rapid technological change adds a second pressure: information flows, cyber dependencies, AI-supported assessments, and digital service infrastructures can change faster than institutional mandates, staff capabilities, oversight rules, and public accountability mechanisms.

The policy implication is not to respond impulsively or to lower evidentiary standards. It is to build **preparedness before crisis**, establish proportionate action thresholds, preserve human judgment, and make non-response visible and reviewable. Waiting for perfect certainty can be dangerous; acting on unverified claims can also cause harm. Institutions need structured uncertainty management, not paralysis or overreaction.

## 2. The institutional problem

Many peace and development institutions operate through separate mandates, funding streams, data systems, geographical jurisdictions, and political reporting lines. Local warnings may be collected by civil society or humanitarian actors but not reach the relevant authority. International bodies may possess formal mandates but lack rapid field-level access or sustained financing. Technology providers may control infrastructure essential to civilian services while remaining outside traditional peacebuilding coordination. These are hypotheses to assess in each context, not blanket judgments about all institutions.

The proposal identifies six recurring institutional failure modes for audit:
1. **Mandate fragmentation:** no single body owns the end-to-end civilian protection referral.
2. **Communication failure:** affected populations cannot safely report harm or receive a response.
3. **Information silos:** data cannot be responsibly shared, interpreted, or challenged across institutions.
4. **Decision opacity:** authority and accountability are unclear, especially across public-private and international-national boundaries.
5. **Response latency:** acknowledgement, assessment, and protective action are delayed without transparent reasons.
6. **Innovation lag:** technology adoption outpaces governance, training, safeguards, and independent evaluation.

## 3. Proposed institutional redesign: a Civilian Protection and Prevention Architecture

### Pillar A — Civilian-facing communication bridges
- Establish safe, multilingual, accessible, low-bandwidth and offline reporting routes.
- Support trusted local intermediaries, independent civil society, women-led and youth-led organizations, disability organizations, and community-based protection networks.
- Provide clear information about what institutions can and cannot do, expected response times, confidentiality limits, and complaint pathways.
- Ensure that channel users are not exposed to retaliation, profiling, or unnecessary data collection.

### Pillar B — Atrocity-risk and protection signal integration
- Use the UN Framework of Analysis for Atrocity Crimes as the established reference for atrocity-risk monitoring.
- Integrate locally validated indicators of service disruption, communication-bridge degradation, population displacement, threats to humanitarian access, identity-based targeting, and institutional non-response.
- Distinguish verified facts, credible allegations, contextual signals, and unconfirmed information.
- Record uncertainty and alternative explanations.

### Pillar C — Accountable decision topology
- Designate an accountable lead for each protection referral while preserving distributed expertise and local ownership.
- Publish role maps and escalation procedures at an institutional level.
- Require handoff receipts, response deadlines, escalation when deadlines are missed, and documented reasons for delay.
- Provide independent review when responsibility is disputed or no institution accepts a referral.

### Pillar D — Civilian service and digital continuity
- Include health, water, food, shelter, education, energy, telecoms, identity/documentation, and humanitarian information systems in civilian protection continuity planning.
- Require public-interest risk assessments for critical technology providers serving conflict-affected populations.
- Establish continuity, backup, recovery, and human-support pathways when digital systems fail.
- Avoid making access to essential protection dependent on a single platform or automated classification.

### Pillar E — Human-centred AI and information governance
- Treat AI outputs as contestable decision support, never as self-authenticating facts or legal determinations.
- Require provenance, contextual validation, bias assessment, meaningful human review, audit trails, and accessible correction.
- Prohibit automated profiling of civilians as proxies for threat or culpability.
- Ensure that civilian complaints and humanitarian reports are not repurposed for intelligence or coercive enforcement without a lawful basis and stringent safeguards.

### Pillar F — Independent oversight and public accountability
- Create independent civilian-protection review functions with authority to audit delays, referral failures, data misuse, and repeated non-response.
- Publish aggregate performance reports without exposing individuals or sensitive locations.
- Provide safe channels for whistleblowers, humanitarian workers, journalists, and affected communities.

## 4. Immediate, short-term, and medium-term action plan

### Immediate: 0–90 days
1. Appoint an accountable senior prevention lead and cross-agency focal points.
2. Conduct a rapid audit of civilian reporting routes, referral handoffs, response times, and existing protection obligations.
3. Identify high-consequence service and communication dependencies using public and non-sensitive information.
4. Establish an interim escalation protocol for credible, urgent civilian-protection warnings.
5. Convene local civil-society and humanitarian partners under safe participation rules.
6. Issue a non-retaliation, data-minimization, and no-automated-culpability directive.

### Short term: 3–12 months
1. Pilot interoperable, rights-protective referral protocols across local, national, and international partners.
2. Train staff in atrocity-risk indicators, uncertainty communication, trauma-informed engagement, IHL/human-rights boundaries, and AI automation-bias risks.
3. Conduct tabletop exercises on communication breakdown, service outages, contradictory information, and unclear institutional ownership.
4. Introduce response-latency dashboards with aggregate, privacy-preserving metrics.
5. Establish independent review of unresolved or repeatedly delayed referrals.
6. Create rapid technical assistance and flexible funding windows for local protection initiatives.

### Medium term: 12–36 months
1. Reform mandates and financing to reward prevention, not only post-crisis response.
2. Build permanent civilian-protection and digital-resilience capacity into peacebuilding and development programmes.
3. Establish cross-border and regional cooperation protocols for displacement, service continuity, humanitarian access, and information integrity.
4. Commission independent evaluation of the pilot architecture and publish failures as well as successes.
5. Integrate anticipatory governance and BAcc-NOVELTY review gates into procurement, technology adoption, and institutional planning.

## 5. Governance model

A **distributed but accountable** model is recommended. Local actors should retain contextual knowledge and meaningful participation; national institutions should retain public responsibilities; international organizations should support coordination, monitoring, and capacity; independent oversight should test compliance and response quality. Distributed participation must not become an excuse for diffused responsibility. Every urgent referral requires a named institutional owner and an auditable handoff.

A proposed coordination cell should be civilian-protection-led, not intelligence-led. It should not conduct surveillance, identify adversaries, or create operational target lists. Its remit is to coordinate public-interest protection signals, service continuity, referral pathways, and institutional accountability.

## 6. Performance indicators

| Outcome | Indicator | Suggested reporting |
|---|---|---|
| Accessible protection | Share of affected population able to identify a safe reporting route | Disaggregated only where safe and statistically sound |
| Timely acknowledgement | Median and 90th-percentile acknowledgement time | Monthly/quarterly aggregate |
| Referral accountability | Share of referrals with named owner and documented handoff | Quarterly |
| Follow-through | Share of urgent referrals receiving a documented protective assessment | Quarterly; quality audit |
| Civilian trust | Voluntary, locally validated trust measure | Periodic independent survey |
| Service continuity | Duration and population impact of essential-service outages | Incident-based aggregate |
| Equity | Access and response gaps across groups, without stigmatizing labels | Independent fairness review |
| Institutional learning | Corrective actions completed after after-action reviews | Semi-annual |

These indicators measure institutional process and access; they do not measure genocide probability. Any risk assessment must remain grounded in established atrocity-prevention frameworks and expert legal analysis.

## 7. Financing and incentives

Prevention requires predictable, multi-year funding, flexible rapid-response allocations, and direct support to locally led organizations. Funding criteria should reward demonstrated protection outcomes, safe community participation, institutional learning, and continuity—not merely the number of dashboards, reports, or technologies deployed. Procurement should include lifecycle costs, accessibility, interoperability, auditability, data protection, and exit/continuity plans.

## 8. BAcc-NOVELTY policy gate

Before adopting a new technology or institutional innovation, decision-makers should answer: (1) What civilian-protection problem does it solve? (2) What evidence supports its use? (3) Who may be excluded or exposed? (4) Can people contest or correct its outputs? (5) Is there meaningful human responsibility? (6) Can the system be paused, reversed, or replaced safely? (7) Does it strengthen local capacity and Positive Peace? (8) What independent evidence will trigger revision or termination?

This operationalizes BAcc-NOVELTY as a proposed principle of balanced acceleration—not as a validated standard.

## 9. Legal and ethical boundaries

This proposal does not confer legal authority on any institution or replace obligations under the Genocide Convention, international humanitarian law, international human-rights law, refugee law, or applicable domestic law. The legal characterization of genocide, crimes against humanity, and war crimes requires competent legal analysis and evidence. Preventive action may and often should be considered before a final legal determination, but must be lawful, proportionate, non-discriminatory, and subject to review.

## 10. Requested institutional decisions

The proposal asks peace-oriented and development institutions to: (a) commission a joint audit of civilian communication bridges and referral latency; (b) establish a named cross-institutional owner for urgent civilian protection referrals; (c) pilot the proposed architecture in selected contexts with local partners; (d) allocate flexible prevention funding; and (e) commission independent evaluation within 12–18 months.

**Core policy conclusion:** preventing atrocity crimes requires more than warning systems. It requires a reliable path from civilian voice to accountable decision and protective action. Inaction, fragmented mandates, and unreviewed delay must be treated as governance risks that institutions can identify, measure, and correct.
