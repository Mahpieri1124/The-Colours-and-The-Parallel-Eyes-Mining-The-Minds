# Research Map, Conceptual Architecture, and Methodology

## 1. Research aim

To develop and empirically examine a civilian-centred, systems-oriented framework for understanding how fragmentation, uncertainty, and disrupted communication between civilians and decision-making institutions may compound civilian vulnerability and obstruct prevention of atrocity crimes.

## 2. Scope and unit of analysis

The primary unit is not an individual suspected actor or an operational command node. It is the **civilian protection and response ecosystem** within a defined locality, service system, or institutional setting. Units may include community-to-service pathways, humanitarian referral networks, local/national/international coordination arrangements, and public-interest information systems.

## 3. Construct map

| Construct | Working definition | Candidate observable dimensions | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Civilian compression | Cumulative constraints on safety, agency, service access, trusted information, and recourse | unmet protection requests; service interruption; displacement pressure; perceived uncertainty; safe reporting access | Proposed |
| Fragmentation | Misalignment or breakdown across social, informational, administrative, and decision systems | referral discontinuity; duplicated mandates; conflicting instructions; coordination gaps | Established general phenomenon; project-specific operationalization proposed |
| Communication-bridge integrity | Whether civilians can safely reach a responsible and responsive institution | reachability; trust; language access; confidentiality; response acknowledgement; closure rate | Proposed integration |
| Decision topology | Distribution and practical exercise of authority, information, and accountability | decision rights; escalation path; ownership; hand-off count; accountability clarity | Analytical lens |
| Response latency | Time between a credible protection signal and an appropriate first institutional response | median time; percentile distribution; delays by population group | Measurable candidate indicator |
| Information integrity | Reliability, provenance, contextualization, and contestability of information | corroboration; source diversity; correction time; uncertainty disclosure | Established field; integrated here |
| Atrocity-risk context | Conditions and indicators relevant to atrocity-crime prevention | UN Framework risk factors and context-specific indicators | Established framework |
| BAcc-NOVELTY | Balance innovation speed with evidence, rights, oversight, and institutional readiness | safeguards present; review gates; reversibility; learning cycles | Author-developed principle; pre-empirical |

## 4. Multilevel architecture

**Level A — Civilian lived experience:** safety, agency, access to essential services, mobility, trusted information, ability to seek assistance, perceived and experienced coercion.

**Level B — Community bridges:** family/community support, local civil society, community-led warning, trusted intermediaries, local media, legal and psychosocial support.

**Level C — Service and humanitarian systems:** health, water, food, shelter, education, protection, humanitarian access, digital and communications infrastructure.

**Level D — Public institutions:** local and national authorities, courts, ombudspersons, emergency management, independent oversight, public accountability.

**Level E — International ecosystem:** UN entities, regional organizations, humanitarian agencies, peacebuilding bodies, development institutions, donor systems, civil society coalitions.

**Cross-cutting layer — Technology and information:** platforms, telecoms, data systems, AI-supported analysis, cyber resilience, information integrity, privacy and security.

The levels are connected through bidirectional feedback and feed-forward relationships. A local warning can travel upward; institutional action or failure travels downward; service disruptions can feed back into trust and reporting; technology can improve connectivity or create new exclusion and exposure.

## 5. Research questions and hypotheses

Use the six questions and five propositions in the HMC-GATE review as the core test set. Add context-specific questions only after consultation with affected communities and local partners.

## 6. Work packages

### WP1 — Conceptual clarification and prior-art review
- Establish a controlled vocabulary and distinguish established legal/policy terms from author-developed constructs.
- Conduct a scoping review across atrocity prevention, human security, hybrid threats, civilian harm, communication access, institutional trust, digital harms, and peacebuilding.
- Publish search strings, dates, languages, databases, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and a PRISMA-ScR-style flow diagram where appropriate.
- Conduct terminology and novelty review for “civilian compression” and “communication bridges.”

### WP2 — Participatory model development
- Convene ethically supported workshops with local civil-society, humanitarian, disability, women-led, youth-led, minority, and community representatives.
- Validate whether the proposed constructs reflect lived experience or impose an external framing.
- Use safe, non-identifying scenarios rather than collecting operationally sensitive details.

### WP3 — Indicator and measurement design
- Develop a small set of process indicators before composite indices.
- Distinguish availability of a communication channel from safety, trust, accessibility, responsiveness, and remedy.
- Define denominator, time window, missing-data handling, disaggregation, and uncertainty for each indicator.

### WP4 — Comparative case studies
- Select cases using transparent criteria: variation in institutional fragmentation, service dependency, communication conditions, and documented civilian protection challenges.
- Use public records, policy documents, humanitarian situation reports, interviews where safe, and process tracing.
- Do not publish sensitive network maps, personal data, or details that could expose sources or communities.

### WP5 — Institutional readiness and response design
- Map public-facing mandates, escalation protocols, referral pathways, and accountability mechanisms.
- Test tabletop scenarios that simulate communication failures and ambiguous responsibility without reproducing tactical adversary playbooks.
- Measure response latency, clarity of ownership, accessibility, and corrective learning.

### WP6 — Evaluation and revision
- Pre-register hypotheses where feasible.
- Use independent ethics and methods review.
- Publish null findings and construct failures.
- Revise or retire constructs that do not demonstrate discriminant validity or practical value.

## 7. Candidate indicators

| Domain | Indicator | Operational definition (illustrative) | Safeguard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reachability | Safe channel availability | Share of surveyed participants who can identify at least one safe, accessible route to seek protection | Never publish identities or precise contact patterns |
| Responsiveness | Acknowledgement latency | Time from receipt of a non-identifying protection request to acknowledgement | Report aggregated distributions only |
| Follow-through | Referral completion | Share of referrals receiving a documented, appropriate next step | Do not equate closure with satisfactory outcome |
| Trust | Perceived channel trust | Voluntary survey measure with validated local adaptation | Avoid coercive or government-linked recruitment |
| Service continuity | Essential-service disruption | Duration and population-level reach of service interruption | Use humanitarian data standards |
| Coordination | Handoff integrity | Proportion of referrals with clear receiving responsibility | No sensitive node-level vulnerability mapping |
| Information quality | Correction and provenance practice | Presence and timeliness of corrections and uncertainty labels | Protect journalists and sources |
| Institutional readiness | Exercise performance | Improvement across repeated scenario exercises | Avoid operational attack scenarios |

These are candidate indicators, not a validated index. A composite “civilian compression score” should not be built until reliability, validity, fairness, and cross-context comparability are demonstrated.

## 8. Methodological design

A mixed-method, sequential design is recommended: (1) scoping review; (2) participatory construct elicitation; (3) indicator development; (4) comparative case studies; (5) process tracing; (6) quantitative analysis of aggregated response and service indicators where ethically available; and (7) institutional scenario evaluation. Triangulate sources and explicitly document disagreement.

Possible quantitative techniques include survival analysis for response latency, multilevel models for locality/institutional variation, network analysis of *publicly documented institutional referral pathways* (not clandestine actor networks), and sensitivity analysis for missing data. Qualitative techniques include framework analysis, contribution analysis, and process tracing. Causal claims require a defensible identification strategy and should not be inferred from correlation.

## 9. Ethics, protection, and data governance

- Do no harm; prioritize safety over data completeness.
- Obtain informed, voluntary consent where human-subject research is conducted.
- Do not collect names, precise locations, affiliations, political beliefs, or contact graphs unless strictly necessary and approved.
- Apply data minimization, encryption, access controls, retention limits, and secure deletion protocols.
- Conduct threat modelling focused on participant exposure and misuse of findings.
- Do not infer culpability or group risk from ethnicity, religion, nationality, political opinion, or protected identity.
- Separate public-interest early warning from surveillance or intelligence collection.
- Ensure independent review and a safe correction/complaint mechanism.
- Follow applicable research ethics rules, IHL, human-rights law, data-protection law, and humanitarian principles.

## 10. Validation gates

1. **Conceptual validity:** experts and affected communities agree that constructs are distinguishable and meaningful.
2. **Content validity:** indicators cover intended dimensions without excessive burden.
3. **Reliability:** coding and measurement are sufficiently consistent.
4. **Construct validity:** proposed measures relate to relevant established measures without simply duplicating them.
5. **Predictive restraint:** test whether indicators provide useful early signals; do not claim prediction unless externally validated.
6. **Fairness and safety:** no systematic stigmatization, disproportionate exposure, or discriminatory effects.
7. **Actionability:** institutions can use findings to improve civilian protection without requiring intrusive data collection.

## 11. Timeline and milestones (indicative)

- Months 4–6: participatory construct review and measurement protocol.
- Months 7–12: pilot case studies and indicator feasibility.
