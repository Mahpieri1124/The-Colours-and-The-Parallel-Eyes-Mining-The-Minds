# Policy Proposal
## Rebuilding the Adaptive Capacity of Global Peace and Sustainable-Development Institutions for the Age of Hybrid Conflict and Advanced Technologies

### Executive summary

Hybrid conflict is no longer adequately understood as a sequence of conventional military events. Contemporary competition can combine military force, cyber operations, information manipulation, economic coercion, technological dependencies, proxy relationships, political interference, and attacks or disruptions affecting civilian infrastructure. AI-enabled systems can accelerate the collection and processing of information and influence the tempo of consequential decisions. These developments create a pressing challenge for peace-oriented institutions: the environment they must understand and help stabilize may change faster than their mandates, analytical practices, coordination mechanisms, and accountability systems.

This proposal calls for a foundational but evidence-led renewal of global peace and sustainable-development institutions. It does not argue that existing organizations are irrelevant or should be discarded. It argues that their operating models, information architectures, institutional interfaces, and learning cycles must be reviewed against the realities of rapidly evolving hybrid conflict and advanced technologies.

The proposed response integrates:
- dynamic, public-interest mapping of decision authority and influence;
- civilian-centred hybrid-conflict analysis;
- information integrity and uncertainty governance;
- HMC-GATE as a structured second-order review;
- BAcc-NOVELTY as a principle for balanced institutional innovation;
- stronger links between conflict prevention, humanitarian protection, Positive Peace, and sustainable development.

### 1. Why action is urgent

#### Short term: immediate civilian exposure

Cyber disruption, harmful information, attacks on essential services, interference with humanitarian operations, and technology-enabled escalation can affect civilians before institutions complete conventional assessment cycles. Health services, communications, energy, transport, finance, education, and humanitarian access may be exposed to direct or cascading disruption. Public trust can be damaged by both hostile manipulation and inaccurate institutional responses.

#### Medium term: institutional adaptation gap

As AI and other advanced technologies become more integrated into military, intelligence, administrative, and information systems, the speed of change may outpace the capacity of international and national institutions to understand and govern their consequences. Decision processes can become faster while accountability, independent review, and meaningful human deliberation remain underdeveloped.

#### Long term: erosion of the conditions of Positive Peace

Persistent insecurity, distrust, unequal exposure to harm, weakened public services, information fragmentation, and unaccountable power can undermine the institutional and social conditions associated with peaceful societies. Peacebuilding must therefore address not only the cessation of violence but also the resilience and legitimacy of the systems on which peaceful life depends.

### 2. The institutional problem

Many peace-oriented organizations were designed around mandates, reporting cycles, categories, and coordination arrangements that may not fully capture:
- rapidly changing combinations of state and non-state actors;
- distributed or semi-centralized decision authority;
- public–private dependencies in digital and critical infrastructure;
- AI-supported information analysis and decision support;
- transnational information and influence networks;
- cumulative civilian harms that cross sectoral and national boundaries;
- uncertainty and contested attribution;
- the need for rapid but rights-respecting institutional learning.

The issue is not simply a lack of technology. It is a systems-governance challenge involving mandates, incentives, data access, expertise, legitimacy, accountability, and coordination.

### 3. Proposed institutional renewal

#### Pillar I — Hybrid Conflict and Civilian Impact Observatory

Create or strengthen an independent, multidisciplinary capability that synthesizes publicly available and lawfully shared information about hybrid-conflict patterns and civilian consequences. It should:
- monitor disruptions to essential civilian services;
- document verified and credibly assessed information with confidence levels;
- distinguish facts, allegations, analysis, and uncertainty;
- connect technical, humanitarian, legal, social, economic, and environmental perspectives;
- protect sources and avoid publishing sensitive operational details;
- provide accessible summaries for affected communities and policymakers.

This is a civilian-protection and peacebuilding observatory, not a clandestine intelligence service.

#### Pillar II — Dynamic Decision-Architecture Assessment

Develop a recurring institutional assessment of where formal authority, practical influence, technical control, information access, and accountability reside. The assessment should distinguish centralized, semi-centralized, decentralized, and dynamically reconfiguring arrangements.

Its purpose is to identify governance gaps and accountability pathways—not exploitable vulnerabilities, targeting opportunities, or covert operational structures.

#### Pillar III — Civilian Digital and Infrastructure Protection

Establish stronger coordination among peacebuilding, humanitarian, development, telecommunications, health, energy, transport, and cybersecurity stakeholders. Priorities should include:
- continuity of essential services;
- protection of medical and humanitarian systems;
- incident communication and public warning;
- privacy and data protection;
- resilient recovery planning;
- safeguards for civilian data and digital infrastructure;
- accessible remedies and reporting channels.

#### Pillar IV — AI and Advanced-Technology Governance for Peace

Peace institutions should maintain the expertise to assess AI-enabled decision systems and other advanced technologies relevant to conflict prevention and civilian protection. Core requirements include:
- meaningful human judgement and accountability;
- reliable and context-appropriate data;
- independent testing and evaluation;
- clear limits on automation;
- bias and error monitoring;
- documentation and auditability;
- human-rights and humanitarian-law review;
- after-action learning and public-interest oversight.

#### Pillar V — HMC-GATE institutional review

Introduce a second-order review process that asks institutions to scrutinize their own problem definitions, assumptions, evidence standards, incentives, blind spots, and distribution of risk. The process should include independent challenge, local knowledge, affected-community participation, and safe correction mechanisms.

#### Pillar VI — BAcc-NOVELTY implementation principle

Institutional reform should neither accelerate technology adoption indiscriminately nor delay useful innovation indefinitely. The pace of change should be proportionate to evidence, uncertainty, reversibility, safeguards, human agency, institutional readiness, and capacity to learn.

### 4. A proposed institutional operating cycle

**Detect → corroborate → classify uncertainty → map decision authority → assess civilian consequences → conduct HMC-GATE review → select proportionate response under BAcc-NOVELTY → coordinate lawful action → monitor → learn → revise.**

This cycle is iterative rather than linear. New evidence or civilian reporting can reopen earlier assessments. A decision may be to act, conduct a bounded pilot, gather evidence, redesign, pause, or stop.

### 5. Governance and institutional safeguards

Any new capability should be:
- impartial and independent in its analytical function;
- transparent about methods, evidence, uncertainty, and limitations;
- accountable to an identifiable governance body;
- subject to independent legal, ethical, and data-protection review;
- inclusive of local peacebuilders, civil society, women, youth, and affected communities;
- protected against partisan capture and conflicts of interest;
- prohibited from profiling populations on protected or sensitive identity grounds;
- designed to avoid duplication of existing UN, regional, humanitarian, and civil-society mandates.

### 6. Implementation roadmap

**Phase 1 — Mandate and feasibility review (0–6 months):** map existing institutional capabilities, identify gaps, consult affected communities, and commission an independent legal and ethical assessment.

**Phase 2 — Design and partnership (6–12 months):** agree on governance, data standards, civilian-impact indicators, independent review, and pilot scope.

**Phase 3 — Bounded pilot (12–24 months):** test the observatory and HMC-GATE/BAcc-NOVELTY protocol in a carefully selected civilian-protection or peacebuilding context.

**Phase 4 — Independent evaluation (18–30 months):** assess timeliness, accuracy, civilian relevance, rights compliance, legitimacy, cost, unintended effects, and added value over existing systems.

**Phase 5 — Replication or redesign (30 months onward):** expand only where independent evidence supports effectiveness, legitimacy, and transferability.

### 7. Evaluation framework

Evaluation should assess:
- time from verified signal to institutional recognition;
- quality and traceability of evidence;
- accuracy of uncertainty statements;
- time to protective coordination;
- continuity and recovery of essential civilian services;
- accessibility of complaint and correction mechanisms;
- participation and trust among affected communities;
- accountability for decisions and errors;
- prevention of duplicate or contradictory institutional responses;
- contribution to selected Positive Peace conditions.

No single index should be treated as a proxy for peace. Results must be disaggregated where safe and interpreted in context.

### 8. Risks and mitigation

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Politicization or partisan capture | Independent governance, plural oversight, transparent methods, conflict-of-interest rules |
| Mission creep into intelligence or surveillance | Explicit non-operational mandate, data minimization, prohibition on covert collection and targeting |
| False attribution | Confidence levels, corroboration, correction procedures, independent review |
| Privacy and source-protection harms | Strict access controls, secure retention, anonymization, risk-based publication |
| Duplication of existing organizations | Mandate mapping, subsidiarity, partnership, shared standards |
| Over-automation | Human accountability, contestability, testing, limits on automated decisions |
| Innovation paralysis | BAcc-NOVELTY staged action, review deadlines, bounded pilots |
| Unsafe acceleration | Reversibility tests, safeguards, legal review, stop criteria |

### 9. The case for foundational reform

A foundational review is necessary because incremental technical upgrades cannot resolve fragmented mandates, unclear accountability, weak interfaces between institutions, or the absence of civilian-centred feedback loops. Yet reform should be evolutionary and evidence-led: identify what works, preserve it, connect it more effectively, and redesign what demonstrably fails.

The 2025 UN Peacebuilding Architecture Review and the 2026 review of UN peace operations provide important institutional contexts for this debate. This proposal should be positioned as a research-informed contribution to ongoing reform discussions, not as a substitute for intergovernmental processes or existing mandates.

### 10. Conclusion

The accelerating convergence of advanced technologies and hybrid conflict creates a short- and medium-term imperative to improve how peace institutions recognize, interpret, and respond to civilian risks. A peace system that cannot understand changing decision architectures, information dependencies, and technology-enabled influence may respond too late—or respond in ways that undermine the very values it is meant to protect.

The proposed module offers a research pathway: dynamic hybrid analysis, civilian-centred evidence, HMC-GATE, and BAcc-NOVELTY, linked to Positive Peace and sustainable development. Its value must be established through transparent comparison, careful pilots, independent evaluation, and revision.

**The objective is not to make peace institutions more militarized. It is to make them more perceptive, accountable, adaptive, and capable of protecting human life and the conditions of peaceful development.**
